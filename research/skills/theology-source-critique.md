# Theology source critique

## Name the kind of authority

Every theological claim rests on one of these, and they are not interchangeable:

- **Scripture** — cite book, chapter and verse, and name the translation, since
  translations differ where the argument turns.
- **Tradition** — the Fathers, the liturgies, the councils. Cite the work, the
  book and chapter, and the edition or translation actually consulted.
- **Magisterial teaching** — cite the document, its date, its level of
  authority (dogmatic definition, encyclical, instruction, private revelation).
  An encyclical and a dogmatic constitution do not weigh the same.
- **Scholarship** — cite the author, the work and the year. A scholar's reading
  of a council is not the council.
- **Practice and custom** — describe it as it is observed, and date it.

A report that cites "the Church teaches" without one of the five is not a
report; it is a paraphrase of the writer's memory.

## Represent each position in its own terms

State an opponent's view as its best exponent states it, then answer that. A
caricature is refuted by its own author, not by the source. Where traditions
genuinely differ — filioque, papal primacy, the nature of justification, the
number of ecumenical councils — say that they differ, name where, and do not
manufacture a synthesis nobody signed.

## No anachronism

Concepts have histories. Do not read a fifth-century text through a
nineteenth-century category. Where a later term is used to describe an earlier
debate, say so: "what later theology calls X, these texts describe as Y".

## Dates, editions, languages

Councils are numbered and dated. Works are dated by their composition, not their
publication. Note when a translation is contested or when a critical edition
differs from the received text — this is often where a popular claim comes from.

## Proportion

Distinguish what is defined, what is commonly held, what is disputed among
Catholics, and what is one theologian's opinion. Mark each as such. Giving all
four the same tone is the most common failure in generated theology, and the
one most likely to mislead the user.
