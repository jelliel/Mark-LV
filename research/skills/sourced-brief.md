# Sourced brief

## The rule

Every sentence that states a fact ends with the number of the source it came
from: `[3]`. A sentence with two sources carries two numbers: `[3][7]`.

If a sentence has no number, it is one of exactly four things, and it says
which:

- **Inference.** "This suggests …" — the writer's reasoning over the sources.
  Mark it: `(inference)`.
- **Common ground.** Textbook material no reasonable source would dispute.
  Do not dress it in a citation.
- **Uncertain.** `(uncertain)` — sources disagree or are thin.
- **Unknown.** Say so. "No source found for X" is a finding, not a failure.

## What counts as a source

1. Primary: the study, the filing, the specification, the court text, the
   dataset, the manufacturer's own documentation.
2. Reference: encyclopaedias, standards bodies, government statistics.
3. Secondary: reporting and analysis that cites primary material.
4. Tertiary: blog posts, aggregators, SEO pages. Only when 1–3 are silent, and
   never for a number that matters.

A page whose text was not read is not a source. A search snippet is a pointer,
not evidence — if the body could not be fetched, write `(abstract only)` next to
its number.

## Numbers

State the unit, the year and the population. "42%" is unusable; "42% of
first-time buyers in the EU, 2024" is a fact. If two sources report different
figures, report both and name the difference; do not average them.

## Dating

A claim is true as of a date. Put the date on the claim when the fact can
change — prices, versions, office-holders, populations, recommendations.

## Length

Dense beats long. A 700-word report with 20 sourced claims is worth more than
2,000 words with 6. Cut any paragraph that repeats the previous one in different
words.
