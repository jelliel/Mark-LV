# Comparative analysis

## Before comparing

Name the criteria **first**, before looking at which option is likely to win.
Criteria chosen after the evidence is criteria chosen to reach a conclusion.

Good criteria are measurable and matter to the user: cost per year, latency at
the 95th percentile, licence, maintenance status, position on a specific
doctrinal question, evidence class. Bad criteria are adjectives: "powerful",
"modern", "robust".

## The table

One row per option, one column per criterion, one cell per intersection. Every
cell carries a source number or `(unknown)`. A table with empty cells and no
`(unknown)` in them is a table that guessed.

## Symmetry

Each option gets the same depth of treatment. The failure mode of every
comparison is three paragraphs on the option the writer likes and one on the
others. If an option is genuinely under-documented, that is itself the finding
and belongs in the table.

## Normalising

Put the numbers in the same unit and the same year before comparing. A price in
USD and a price in EUR, a benchmark on different hardware, a doctrine defined
before and after a council: these are not comparable until they are normalised,
and the normalisation is part of the answer.

## The conclusion

"No clear winner; A is better if X matters, B if Y does" is a complete and
respectable conclusion. So is "the evidence is insufficient to rank these".
Inventing a winner to sound decisive is the one outcome that is always wrong.

Offer a recommendation only when the user's own constraints make one option
dominant — and then say which constraint decided it.
